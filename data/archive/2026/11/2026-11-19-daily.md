- **Source:** arxiv
- **Type:** paper
- **Category:** cs.CL
- **Summary:** Jailbreak attempts and harmful prompts remain a challenge for the safe deployment of large language models. We study whether contrastively learned representations can support safety detection without training an additional downstream classifier. We propose Jailbreak Detection with Prototype Inference (JDPI), which uses binary safety labels to construct harmful and benign prototypes and makes predictions from relative prototype distances. JDPI uses only binary safety labels for encoder adaptation, prototype construction, and validation. In our setup, it uses fewer labeled examples than the classifier baseline (Method A) by avoiding the separate split used to train the downstream classifier. Across four benchmarks covering different input formats, contrastive fine-tuning improves JDPI's detection accuracy by 9.1-18.0 percentage points. In multi-turn replay on TomGibbs, JDPI uses accumulated user history as the conversation unfolds, achieving higher conversation-level accuracy than when it uses the current turn alone. Across 12 directed transfers, JDPI outperforms the classifier by an average of 1.59 percentage points in accuracy, demonstrating stronger cross-dataset robustness.

### 7. Knowing What You Know: A Trait Theory of Reviewer Value in Human-AI Cognitive Systems
- **Source:** arxiv
- **Type:** paper
- **Category:** cs.AI
- **Summary:** When a capable model and a human reviewer cooperate on the same decision, which human still adds value? We argue this is a question about a single cognitive trait, metacognitive calibration: the alignment between a reviewer's behavioral confidence and the actual correctness of their judgments. We develop a trait theory of reviewer value to make it precise. The theory makes five claims: value is governed by calibration rather than by activity, speed, agreement, or tenure; calibration leaves observable traces in behavioral telemetry; those traces are non-diagnostic on their own, because the fast, low-revision signature of a calibrated expert is indistinguishable from that of a confident novice until it is gated by outcomes; human calibration and a model's self-knowledge are the same construct, so the joint system should defer to whichever agent is better calibrated on each item; and individual calibration is causally identifiable only above the level of the single judgment. We ground each claim in a six-month production window of an AI-assisted accounting system (541621 reviewer events, 483 reviewers), and find that a purely behavioral expertise profile inverts the ground truth, scoring an overconfident cohort (5.2 percent correction survival) higher than genuine experts (99.2 percent); that reviewer quality is flat across tenure, so the trait is not learned on the job; and that the same calibration logic that separates good from bad reviewers, applied to the model, concentrates 92.5 percent of human effort on genuine errors. We close with the boundary the theory must respect: under majority-vote aggregation, individual causal value is unidentifiable.

### 9. Agentic Harness Engineering Beta: Requirement-Centered Evolution of Agent Harnesses
- **Source:** arxiv
- **Type:** paper
- **Category:** cs.SE
- **Summary:** Real-world harness engineering is driven by explicit and implicit user requirements, which guide development and validation. However, existing harness evolution methods often overlook implicit requirements and interaction needs, while using a single evolution agent to modify the harness and benchmark scores to select changes. This process often produces narrow patches and leads to benchmark-specific local optima. We introduce Agentic Harness Engineering Beta (AHE-Beta), a requirement-centered loop that brings real-world engineering practices into harness evolution. The loop consists of three steps: (1) User-driven evaluation lets simulated users raise their requirements as issues while they work with the harness over multiple turns. (2) Team-based modification implements those requirements as generalizable changes rather than task-specific patches. (3) Requirement-driven selection merges a change only when it resolves a verified requirement and passes review. Empirically, on held-out tasks, AHE-Beta raises pass@1 from 21.9% to 33.3% while using 40.8% fewer tokens than Base Harness, and achieves higher pass@1 than Claude Code, Meta-Harness, and AHE. In a blind human A/B test, real users prefer AHE-Beta in 31 of 48 valid pairs and rate it higher on all five dimensions, matching the directional preference of the user simulator. The human A/B results show that AHE-Beta achieves its goal of aligning harness evolution with real-world user requirements. Without further evolution, the gains transfer to GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with pass@1 improvements of 15.8% and 5.3% over Base Harness. Together, these results show that requirement-centered harness evolution is a more general method for improving agent capabilities.

### 10. On Evaluating User Simulators for Interactive Agents
- **Source:** arxiv
- **Type:** paper
- **Category:** cs.LG
- **Summary:** User simulators offer a scalable way to evaluate interactive agents, but how to assess their quality remains underexplored. In particular, it is unclear whether existing user simulator evaluation frameworks reliably detect meaningful failures in simulated user behavior. This uncertainty makes it difficult to interpret simulator evaluation. To understand what a high benchmark score tells us about simulator quality, we (meta-)evaluate the metrics themselves. To instantiate this meta-evaluation, we deliberately perturb simulated replays to induce failures such as altered goals, omitted constraints, premature stopping, and acceptance of broken work, then test whether the metrics penalize these failures. Evaluation methods from three prior studies detect only 5-6 of the 10 perturbations; their combined scores even favor populations instructed to accept broken work over unmodified controls. Motivated by these gaps, we propose a new evaluation suite that combines utility of user simulators for evaluating agents and two measures of fidelity to real users: population-level fidelity assesses collective interaction patterns, while trace-level fidelity assesses the preservation of individual users' goals, constraints, and persona. Under the same detection criterion, our evaluation suite detects all 10 perturbations. Evaluating six user simulators on 351 real multi-turn sessions in which 348 distinct users across 23 languages and agents work together to create and refine artifacts such as full-stack web apps, games, design assets, and automated workflows, we find that population-level measures can miss when a simulated user pursues the wrong goal, ignores a constraint, or behaves out of character, highlighting the need to assess fidelity at comprehensive granularities.
