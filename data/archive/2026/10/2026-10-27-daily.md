- **Source:** arxiv
- **Type:** paper
- **Category:** cs.AI
- **Summary:** While language models have become dramatically more capable, retrieval still largely relies on lexical matching or similarity in fixed embedding spaces. This creates a capability asymmetry: what the retriever fails to find, even the strongest generator cannot recover. We propose DecisionRAG, which brings the capabilities of pretrained language models to retrieval using decision models that jointly evaluate candidate branches in a single forward pass, without generating any tokens, and show that judging candidates in a shared context makes their relevance estimates comparable. Like a person browsing a library, DecisionRAG navigates the corpus's inherent structure from broad topics down to individual passages, deciding at each level which branches are worth exploring. Without task-specific training or a first-stage retriever, DecisionRAG outperforms dense retrieval with an 8B reranker by 13.8 R@1 points on MultiDoc2Dial and 17.4 Hit@1 points on Natural Questions. ### 8. On the stability of learning
- **Source:** arxiv
- **Type:** paper
- **Category:** q-bio.NC
- **Summary:** Learning algorithms are usually analyzed under the assumption that informative gradients are continuously available. But this is not always true---learning signals in biological neural networks, for example, may be sparse, local, and only intermittently available at any given synapse. In this work, we show that if gradient information is noisy and only intermittently available, learning may be unstable. In particular, even if average learning dynamics converge to the correct optimum, variance in learning may be asymptotically infinite. We explore this point in a number of analytically tractable settings, including single-layer and multi-layer linear networks, and derive explicit conditions under which learning is stable. These conditions complement, but are conceptually distinct from, existing conditions related to the stability of stochastic gradient descent. Our work demonstrates that unbiased credit assignment is not enough for reliable learning; sufficiently frequent and informative feedback is also required, which yields an additional constraint on models of biologically plausible learning.
